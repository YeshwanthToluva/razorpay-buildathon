00 · Cold open~20s · camera on you

[Screen: the Live tab, nothing running yet. Sit still. Do not rush this one.]

A payment fails. A customer walks away from a checkout. An invoice goes past due.

Every one of those is revenue that already exists and is about to stop existing.

The obvious fix is to hand it to an AI agent. The obvious problem is that you have just given a language model access to money.

[beat]

So we built the agent. And then we spent most of our time trying to prove it could not be trusted.

01 · What it is~40s

[Screen: scroll to “01 How it works”.]

This is a revenue recovery agent. It detects revenue at risk, decides the right intervention, and executes a bounded recovery workflow.

Three kinds of risk: a failed payment, an abandoned checkout, an overdue receivable.

Six actions it can take — retry the payment, schedule a retry, generate a payment link, send a reminder, request a human, stop recovery. That list is closed. If the model invents a seventh, it is rejected before anything else is even considered.

02 · The one idea~50s · the core of the pitch

[Screen: the architecture strip. Slow down here. This is the whole thesis.]

Here is the only architectural idea that matters.

The model never executes anything. It proposes.

Between the proposal and the money sits a deterministic policy engine — 15 rules, evaluated in a fixed order, first refusal wins, and the list ends in default-deny. Pure functions. No I/O. No model.

And it is not a convention — it is the type system. The provider that moves money will only accept an AuthorizedAction, and an AuthorizedAction can only be minted inside the policy module. There is no other constructor.

If any code tries to build one elsewhere, it does not misbehave — it raises PolicyBypassError. You cannot forget to check the policy, because you cannot call the provider without the object that only the policy can produce.

[beat]

Memory is what the agent knows. Policy is what it is allowed to do. Knowing something is never permission to do it.

03 · Live run~2m · the demo

[RISK = failed_retry_then_recovery (pay_0042, ₹1,776) · CHANNEL = Real email · AGENT = Reasoning agent → press Detect & recover. Narrate the stages as they land. If it lags, stop talking and let it land.]

Let me just run one.

One. It detects the revenue at risk — the amount, why it is unpaid, and whether we are already owed the money or about to lose the sale entirely.

Two. The agent decides. That is the model — its diagnosis, its chosen action, its confidence.

Three. The policy engine rules on it. Watch this line: it names the exact rule and the reason. Allowed, refused, or held for a human.

Four. Now the part I actually want you to see. The agent writes the email itself — and everything it writes goes through a second boundary before it can leave.

[If the content policy refuses it, the refused draft is on screen struck through — stop and point at it. That is the best moment in the demo. If it passes, say the next line instead.]

A content policy — eight rules over open prose. It refuses a promise nobody authorised. It refuses an amount that is not the amount owed. It refuses collections pressure. It refuses anything that reads like phishing. It refuses internal jargon leaking into a customer's inbox.

Five. A real email leaves — with a real payment link.

[Switch to your inbox. Show the mail. Click the link. Pay.]

I pay it. And the agent closes the loop — it observes the payment, marks the revenue recovered, and sends the receipt.

Notice what it did not do while it was waiting. It did not retry. It did not message me again. It did not guess. It parked the case until real money actually arrived.

04 · The tools~35s · Composio

[Screen: the trace panel — the tool span. Or the Composio dashboard.]

The email is real. It goes out over Composio's Gmail MCP tool, on my own connected account, over their REST API.

And this is where most agent demos quietly cheat. A Gmail toolkit can read your mail, list your contacts, manage your drafts. Our agent gets exactly one action: GMAIL_SEND_EMAIL. One entry in an allowlist. Anything else raises ToolScopeError.

And the recipient? That is not checked by the mail tool. It is checked by the policy engine — because a tool that validates its own recipients is a convention, and a rule the gateway consults before minting authority is a boundary.

05 · The evidence~70s · the strongest section

[Screen: Safety tab. Then click a blocked attempt to open the modal.]

So: does the boundary actually hold? We have receipts.

Across the runs, the agent proposed 137 actions that the policy engine did not authorise.

113 were refused outright. 24 were high-value and held for a human — and at this autonomy level there is no human, so they did not execute either.

Actions that reached the provider without an approval: zero. Not low. Zero. Every single run.

[Click one blocked attempt. The modal re-runs it against the live engine.]

And these are not screenshots. Click any refusal and it re-runs that exact request against the live policy engine right now — the verdict, the deciding rule, the full list of rules consulted, and the fact that no AuthorizedAction was ever minted.

Retry cooldown stopped 38. Retry limit, 33. High value, 24. Expired recovery window, 21. Fraud hold, 5.

The agent tried. The rulebook said no. Every time, on the record.

06 · What it does that rules cannot~55s

[Screen: chapter 02 — the message comparison, template on the left, agent on the right.]

Now — if the policy engine is that strict, why have an agent at all?

Here is the same failed payment, written two ways.

The rule set: “We were unable to collect one thousand seven hundred and seventy six rupees for invoice inv double-oh forty-two, because your bank declined the payment.” Correct. Mechanical. Identical for every customer who ever hits that code.

The agent, on the same case: it says this was the first attempt, and a second try often goes through. It offers a way out if the card was replaced. It invites a reply.

That is the line a template cannot write, because a template does not know which case it is in. Nine lifetime payments, five successful, one decline. That is not a debtor — that is a good customer with a bank that hiccupped, and the message should sound like it.

The rule set governs what may happen. The agent governs how it reads. And the content policy makes the second one safe enough to ship.

07 · The honest part~55s · do not skip this

[Screen: the docs folder on GitHub, or the experiments list.]

One more thing, and it is the part I would want to hear if I were you.

We ran 21 recorded runs and wrote 4 experiment records. Two of them falsified our own hypotheses.

We thought the agent would recover more revenue than a fixed rule set. It did not. The rule set won on recovery rate, and we published that.

We thought the agent was failing because it lacked context. So we gave it perfect context. Context fidelity went up sharply. Revenue did not move.

We thought it was failing because it never learned its retries failed. So we fed every outcome back. Absorption went from 47% to 87%. Revenue did not move — to the rupee.

[beat]

Two experiments improved what the agent knew and neither moved the outcome. That is real evidence that the remaining gap is not an information problem — and it is why the next experiment is a different one. That is in the repo too, with the negative results kept in.

08 · Close~30s

[Screen: the repo. Then back to camera for the last two lines.]

Everything is public. The dataset, hash-pinned. The audit ledger, append-only, enforced by database triggers. Every execution trace. Every refusal. 375 tests — 208 of them on the policy engine alone, 19 adversarial tests where a deliberately hostile agent tries to get something dangerous past the boundary.

It never does.

[beat]

So — can an AI agent recover revenue?

It can. But that was never the hard part.

The hard part is building the thing so that when the model is wrong — and it will be wrong — being wrong is not the same as being dangerous.

That is what we built. Thank you.

Q&A · backup factsnot part of the take

Data is fully synthetic — no real customer, payment or communication data, and no real money moves. The provider is a simulator.

Email allowlist is hard-coded to one address and enforced in the policy engine, not the mail tool. Empty allowlist denies everyone — a misconfigured deploy sends nothing, not everything.

Simulator outcomes are a keyed hash of seed, payment, action and attempt — so a result never depends on the order cases ran in. Same seed, same answer.

Policy config is fingerprinted over the thresholds AND the rule list, so adding a rule changes the fingerprint. Every run records the fingerprint it ran under.

Two orthogonal state variables: payment_state is financial truth, recovery_state is orchestration. Money in the ledger is integer paise, never a float.

Autonomy Level 2: model proposes, deterministic policy disposes, no human in the loop. Escalation is therefore terminal — which is exactly why the agent's over-escalation cost it revenue, and we measured that at 94.6% of the gap.
